1) I read the new version of the EOI, but it is quite incomplete. It would be good to provide a table of contents and a complete outline, to make it easier to comment. A section specifying the objectives of the project is needed. It should be indicated, early in the text, what is the appropriate role of the proposed project, i.e., “to provide substantially improved, uncertainty-characterized constraints on phytoplankton carbon, particulate organic carbon, their vertical distributions, and their temporal variability, and to evaluate how assimilating those constraints reduces uncertainty in modeled biological production and carbon export.”. But the project should not claim or imply that the new (improved) satellite products will determine or close the global ocean carbon budget. It will only constrain biological carbon stocks and selected biological fluxes. Improving, and potentially producing a model-based estimate of the complete ocean carbon budget, would require assimilating DIC, alkalinity, and other carbon system observations, and estimating all important external boundary fluxes. 



2) In section II, consider modifying the last paragraph as follows (text includes lidar and glider dimensions): “Even a perfect satellite retrieval of near-surface phytoplankton carbon or bbp would not resolve its vertical distribution. Passive ocean-color radiometry provides a depth-weighted signal from approximately the upper optical depth and generally contains little direct information from near the base of the euphotic zone or below it. Vertical information must therefore come from profiling observations. At global scales, BGC-Argo provides profiles of fluorescence-derived chlorophyll-a, bbp, and, on a subset of floats, downwelling irradiance and other biogeochemical quantities. Large autonomous-glider programs provide higher-resolution regional sections and repeated sampling of fronts, eddies, and coastal systems. Active lidar offers an additional, complementary dimension: historical CALIOP observations have demonstrated sensitivity to subsurface particle and phytoplankton structure, while future spaceborne systems being developed by space agencies, including the NASA-ASI CALIGOLA concept, could provide improved vertically resolved backscatter, fluorescence, and attenuation measurements. However, systematic offsets, differences in sampling scales, sensor-specific biases, and incomplete overlap among satellite, float, glider, and lidar observations have so far limited their quantitative synthesis (e.g., Bisson et al., 2020; Long et al., 2025).



3) In section II consider ending with something like: “Our team will develop a unified framework that combines these complementary observations rather than treating any one observing system as sufficient. We will improve atmospheric correction and radiative-transfer modeling; retrieve optical and biological properties through Bayesian inference with explicit uncertainty; and reconcile satellite, BGC-Argo, glider, lidar, and ship-based measurements using common observation operators and sensor-specific error models. The resulting products will be assimilated into a physical-biogeochemical ocean state estimate to reconstruct the three-dimensional distribution and temporal evolution of phytoplankton carbon and related carbon pools. Independent cruise observations will be used to validate each step, quantify when estimates are controlled by the measurements versus the prior or model, and assess how improved biological constraints reduce uncertainty in primary production, export, carbon storage, and the broader ocean carbon budget.” 



4) You should talk about field program in the proposal, without being specific. Consider including something like: “Existing in situ datasets from prior ocean-color, bio-optical, BGC-Argo, carbon-cycle, and export studies will provide the primary observational basis for the project. Following an initial assessment of their geographic, seasonal, and measurement coverage, targeted field observations may be conducted to fill critical gaps and obtain measurements that are rarely available simultaneously. These observations would include satellite-coincident hyperspectral radiometry, absorption, scattering, and particle-size measurements; phytoplankton carbon, particulate organic carbon, pigments, and community composition, and, where feasible, primary production, carbon export, carbonate-system, and physical measurements. Vertical profiles from shipboard instruments, BGC-Argo floats, and gliders would connect satellite observations of the near-surface ocean to carbon distributions at depth. The field activities would also support sensor intercalibration, evaluation of spatial and temporal representativeness, and independent validation of the retrievals and their uncertainties. Rather than constituting a new global survey, this targeted effort would complement existing datasets by providing the coordinated optical, biological, chemical, and physical observations required to test the complete radiance-to-carbon framework."



5) A key component of the project is to provide improved estimates of phytoplankton carbon. This should be explicit in the proposal. Consider the following: “A central objective will be to improve the retrieval of phytoplankton carbon across the full satellite record, recognizing that most heritage sensors provide only multispectral observations. The project will use a common Bayesian inversion of the underlying bio-optical state together with sensor-specific observation operators that account explicitly for each instrument’s spectral-response functions and available bands. PACE and in situ hyperspectral observations will be degraded to the band configurations of SeaWiFS, MODIS, VIIRS, OLCI, and other sensors to determine which carbon-related information is retained, develop transferable relationships, and quantify the information lost by multispectral sampling. Environmental, spatial, and temporal context will provide additional probabilistic constraints. The resulting record will therefore be consistent in its retrieved variables and methodology while carrying sensor-dependent uncertainties that reflect the greater information content of PACE relative to the heritage missions.”



6) Regarding my contribution to the project, here is a statement: “R. Frouin, SIO, will lead the development of a consistent, long-term global record of remote-sensing reflectance, Rrs, extending from SeaWiFS through MODIS, MERIS, VIIRS, OLCI, SGLI, and PACE OCI. The work will place the individual mission products on a common radiometric, atmospheric-correction, spectral, spatial, and temporal basis before merging them, explicitly accounting for differences in spectral-response functions, calibration, sampling, and spatial support. Biases and discontinuities will be characterized during mission-overlap periods and corrected relative to common reference observations, while the merging framework will incorporate sensor-specific random and systematic uncertainties, representativeness errors, and correlations arising from shared algorithms and ancillary data. The resulting product will include per-pixel uncertainty and source-contribution information, enabling users to distinguish well-observed conditions from estimates dependent on a single sensor or limited sampling. Evaluation will use independent in situ radiometry, mission-overlap comparisons, sensor-withholding experiments, simulated historical gaps, and tests of temporal stability and trend preservation. This climate-quality Rrs record will provide the consistent observational foundation needed to retrieve and analyze long-term changes in phytoplankton carbon and other biologically relevant ocean properties.”
