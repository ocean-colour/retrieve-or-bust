Unravel: untangling ocean color with data-constrained models

I. Introduction

The past decade has witnessed the arrival of two game-changing tools for assessing the oceanic carbon cycle: (1) the Phytoplankton, Aerosol, Cloud ocean Ecosystem (PACE) satellite with its Ocean Color Instrument and (2) the BGC-Argo project with its suite of biogeochemical sensors. These global datasets assess the ocean’s constituents at unprecedented levels both across its entire surface (PACE) and to 2000m depth at select locations (BGC-Argo).

This proposal deliberately targets the biological component of the ocean carbon cycle — the carbon stocks and fluxes carried by phytoplankton and particulate matter. In the contemporary anthropogenic carbon inventory, the physical and chemical terms dominate and are comparatively well constrained; the biological stocks and fluxes are now the limiting uncertainty, and they are the terms that physical and carbonate-system programs cannot reach. Our objective is to provide substantially improved, uncertainty-characterized constraints on phytoplankton carbon (Cphyto), particulate organic carbon (POC), their vertical distributions, and their temporal variability, and to evaluate how assimilating those constraints reduces uncertainty in modeled biological production and carbon export. We do not claim to close the global ocean carbon budget; we constrain the biological terms that current budgets treat most poorly.

Success is measurable. We will (i) determine the global phytoplankton carbon stock — today uncertain to a factor of ~3.5 from the optics-to-carbon conversion alone (218–771 Tg C) — to better than 50%, with uncertainties validated against independent methods rather than in-sample statistics; (ii) deliver the corresponding global POC record to the same standard, closing at least half of the 2.5–3.5× disagreement among methods that should agree; and (iii) shorten the time to detect a climate-driven trend in ocean biomass from >30 years to ~20 — within reach of the existing multi-mission record — by demonstrating accuracy gains in the PACE/hyperspectral era and transferring them to the heritage record through a single, physically consistent, cross-calibrated retrieval.

Why is this not already done? Because the measurements, on their own, cannot do it. BGC-Argo has provided a first large-scale evaluation of carbon biomass through particulate backscattering bbp [Stoer+25], yet that estimate is systematically limited by the sparsity of the array, the conversion of bbp to carbon mass [Graff+2015], and the offset between satellite and in-situ bbp [Bisson+20XX]; its record is also far shorter than the multi-decadal baseline that only the satellite ocean-color record provides. Ocean color, in turn, is inherently limited: a physical degeneracy in the radiative transfer [Snyder; Prochaska], surface-only sensitivity, the terrific challenge of atmospheric correction [Frouin+2019], and degeneracies between absorbing components [Houskeeper+2025]. Therefore, despite the billions of dollars invested by NASA and other space agencies, the community has yet to produce an estimate of any optically-constrained biological carbon quantity — phytoplankton carbon, POC, net primary production, export — that is reliable to better than a factor of ~3 (Figure 1); the inorganic system (e.g. DIC) is far better known, which sharpens rather than softens the problem [Werdell+2018; Cael+2023; Prochaska&Frouin 2025].

Are we stuck? Probably — so long as we demand the answer from direct measurements alone. Even targeted campaigns like NASA’s EXPORTS yielded carbon-flux estimates in two isolated regions with ~2x systematic uncertainties [Siegel], and the intentionally simple models introduced to convert data into carbon-cycle measurements [DeVries&Weber 2017; Nowicki+2022] primarily recast the empirical limitations into another form with even greater uncertainty.

Our central hypothesis is that the missing information exists — in the vertical structure measured by floats and gliders, in the process knowledge encoded in ecosystem models, and in the covariances that data assimilation exploits — and that the only path forward is to bring sophisticated ocean models to the data. Concretely, we will meet on common ground: forward-modeling each observation in its own measured quantity — radiances, float profiles, cruise optics — through common observation operators and sensor-specific error models embedded in data-constrained ocean state estimates, rather than comparing derived products of inconsistent provenance. To succeed, this must be performed both in regional systems (e.g. the California coastal current) where the complex processes can be resolved, and globally, where the carbon cycle lives. With these challenges in mind, we have assembled a team of experts spanning the full chain from radiance to carbon — and, in tandem, we will re-evaluate the data to maximize its impact on models and develop the models and assimilation techniques to best leverage the data.

II. Break the carbon cycle degeneracies with ocean modeling

Advanced biogeochemical models simulate the components of the carbon cycle — air-sea exchange, primary production, transfer to higher trophic levels, vertical export, remineralization. Because these models are complex, nonlinear, and rest on imperfect parameterizations, their evolution deviates from nature; data assimilation is how those deviations are corrected, much as in numerical weather prediction. The goal of our modeling component is to improve biogeochemical simulations through assimilation of new data types that specifically constrain the carbon cycle.

We leverage two established, data-constrained systems: the global ECCO ocean state estimate and a regional ROMS implementation for the California Current, each coupled to the Darwin ecosystem model. Darwin resolves multiple phytoplankton functional types and size classes, and — critically for this project — includes explicit spectral radiative transfer, so the model predicts the quantities that satellites and BGC-Argo actually measure (reflectance, backscattering) from taxa- and size-specific absorption and scattering: exactly the diversity at the heart of the degeneracies above. That ecological richness, however, has made adjoint-based assimilation impractical. We will resolve this with D-LITE, a lightened Darwin built from the reduced-complexity NBLING model and Darwin itself: simplifications chosen deliberately to preserve the ecological detail needed for optical closure while enabling an efficient full adjoint — generated for ECCO with the same MITgcm/Tapenade framework that produces ECCO’s physical adjoint, and for ROMS with an established dual-number technique already demonstrated on an 11-component regional ecosystem model.

Coupling D-LITE to Darwin’s radiative-transfer package enables hyperspectral data assimilation: we compare simulated and observed radiances directly, rather than collapsing PACE’s 100+ bands into a handful of derived products first. We will simultaneously assimilate vertically resolved BGC-Argo bio-optical and biogeochemical profiles, SOCAT surface pCO2, glider pH, GLODAP carbon-system and nutrient observations, and both PACE and the historical multispectral record. Machine-learning emulators of the biological components, embedded in an Ensemble 4D-Var framework, make the optimization iterative and fast: as model deficiencies are identified, the forward model improves, the emulators are retrained, and uncertainty shrinks. The two scales work together — the data-rich California Current resolves the submesoscale structure and community variability that drive the CDOM–detritus–phytoplankton degeneracy and validates optical closure against dense in-situ sampling; those calibrated relationships then inform, and are tested against, the global state estimate.

III. A climate-quality observational foundation, anchored in situ

A consistent multi-mission Rrs record. A dedicated radiometry and atmospheric-correction work package will construct a consistent global record of remote-sensing reflectance from SeaWiFS through MODIS, MERIS, VIIRS, OLCI, SGLI, and PACE OCI — placing the missions on a common radiometric, atmospheric-correction, and spectral basis, correcting biases during mission overlaps, and propagating sensor-specific uncertainties into the merged product. This climate-quality record is the observational foundation for detecting long-term change.

Carbon across the full satellite record. A common Bayesian inversion with sensor-specific observation operators will retrieve the bio-optical state across all missions; PACE and in-situ hyperspectral observations, degraded to heritage band configurations, quantify what information the multispectral era retains and carry the PACE-era accuracy gains back through the full record — with sensor-dependent uncertainties that honestly reflect each mission’s information content.

A distributed BGC-Argo observational layer. We will harmonize existing multi-instrument float datasets — core biogeochemical sensors plus particle imaging (UVP), transmissometry, and, where available, hyperspectral radiometry — into interoperable, openly distributed profile products with propagated uncertainties. This layer serves two purposes: quality-controlled, satellite-coincident reflectance across diverse optical regimes, extending validation far beyond fixed reference sites; and co-located particle and biological measurements that connect surface optical signatures to vertically resolved carbon stocks and fluxes. Building on it, we will define the requirements — priority regimes, sampling strategies, uncertainty targets — for a future sustained hyperspectral BGC-Argo sub-fleet.

Targeted field observations. Existing in-situ datasets from prior ocean-color, bio-optical, BGC-Argo, and carbon-cycle studies provide the primary observational basis. Targeted fieldwork over the next two years, from ongoing funded projects, will fill gaps identified in that synthesis: satellite-coincident hyperspectral radiometry, absorption, scattering, pigments and community composition, and export-related vertical profiles. These observations support sensor intercalibration and independent validation of the complete radiance-to-carbon framework — not a new global survey.

IV. Team composition and external partnerships

The team spans the full radiance-to-carbon chain: hyperspectral radiative transfer and Bayesian inversion (the PI’s group); satellite radiometry and atmospheric correction across the multi-mission record; global ocean state estimation and biogeochemical assimilation; regional ocean modeling and data assimilation for the California Current; marine ecosystem modeling and spectral radiative-transfer code development; BGC-Argo multi-instrument data integration and bio-optics, led by a European laboratory at the forefront of float bio-optical observations; float sensor and carbon-system analysis; in-situ bio-optics, phytoplankton ecology, and seagoing field programs; and POC algorithm development. The consortium is international and multidisciplinary, several work packages are led by early-career researchers, and the project builds on — and returns products to — community infrastructure including BGC-Argo/OneArgo, the NASA ocean-color processing system, and the ECCO consortium.

V. Timeline and preliminary budget

Over a five-year performance period, years 1–2 deliver the harmonized in-situ datasets, the D-LITE model and its adjoints, and the multi-mission retrieval framework; years 2–4 execute the satellite reprocessing and the regional and global assimilation; years 4–5 deliver the uncertainty-quantified global carbon-state products, the trend analysis, and synthesis. The preliminary budget is approximately $8.9M: predominantly personnel across the work packages above, plus cloud compute and storage for the multi-mission reprocessing, ship time and field observations through leveraged ongoing projects, BGC-Argo data processing, an annual science-team meeting, and open-data publication. Indirect costs are capped at 10% per Schmidt Sciences policy.

VI. Relation to the Schmidt portfolio, data openness, and use of generative AI

The project complements, without duplicating, the existing Schmidt carbon portfolio: COCO2 constrains Southern Ocean air–sea CO2 exchange; InMOS integrates models and observations across scales; SUBSEA targets subsurface export biogeochemistry; CLARiTy demonstrates the EO+AI pattern for the land sink. None delivers the uncertainty-quantified, satellite-based record of the ocean’s biological carbon stocks that we propose, and we will coordinate with all four. Uncertainty-quantified biological carbon stocks are also a prerequisite for observation-based monitoring, reporting, and verification of marine carbon dioxide removal and for treaty-relevant ocean monitoring. All data products will be openly published under FAIR principles. Generative AI (Anthropic’s Claude) was used as a drafting, editing, and literature-synthesis assistant in preparing this proposal; all scientific content, decisions, and claims are the team’s own.
